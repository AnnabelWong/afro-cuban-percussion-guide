Explore four influential Cuban traditions, then meet batá in its distinct Lucumí religious and musical context. These traditions overlap, but each has its own history and rhythmic practices.
Pillar 1 — The People's Music
Rumba Cubana
A secular genre of Cuban music involving dance, percussion, and song. Originated in the solares (courtyard tenements) and dockyards of Havana and Matanzas during the late 19th century, created by free and enslaved Black Cubans. Percussion-driven, sung in call-and-response, and built on the rumba clave.
  • Yambú — the oldest and slowest style. Movements feign frailty. Danced solo (usually by women) or as a couple, but without the vacunao.
  • Guaguancó — the most popular style. Medium tempo. A couple dance of flirtatious competition: the man attempts the vacunao (pelvic thrust), the woman deflects with skirt movements and turns.
  • Columbia — fast and virtuosic, traditionally a solo male dance in 6/8 (12/8). Grounded in Abakuá traditions. The quinto drummer must respond instantly to the dancer's steps.
Core drums: quinto (lead, highest), tres dos (middle), tumba/salidor (lowest). In columbia, a bell or guataca (hoe blade) provides the timeline.

Pillar 2 — The Sound of Carnival
Comparsa — Carnival Percussion
Comparsas are large ensembles of musicians, singers, and dancers with specific costumes and choreography that perform in the street carnivals of Santiago de Cuba, Camagüey, and Havana. The term conga refers to both the music groups and the music they play. Their traditions date to the 19th century, descending from cabildos de nación — mutual aid societies organized by African ethnic groups.
  • Congas santiagueras (Santiago): Features the corneta china (Chinese cornet — a piercing reed instrument introduced by Chinese immigrants), bocúes (single-headed open drums similar to ashikos), quinto, galletas, pilón, and brake drums struck with metal sticks.
  • Congas habaneras (Havana): No corneta china. Uses trumpets, trombones, and saxophones. Percussion includes redoblantes (side drums), bombos (bass drums), quinto, tumbadora, and metallic idiophones (cowbells, spoons, frying pans).
Conga music was introduced into Cuban popular music in the early 20th century by Eliseo Grenet and later influenced salsa and songo.

Shared roots: an institution, not only a rhythm

Abakuá is a Cuban male initiatory and mutual-aid institution rooted in the Cross River region of present-day southeastern Nigeria and southwestern Cameroon. Enslaved Africans and their descendants recreated aspects of Ékpè in Cuba through community organization, religious practice, song and dance. Carabalí cabildos provided an important social setting for this development.

The first Cuban lodge, Efik Butón, is associated with Regla in 1836. Havana, Matanzas and Cárdenas became important centers. Abakuá should therefore be introduced through both Havana and Matanzas, rather than presented as exclusively Havanese. Read Ivor Miller’s historical introduction; see also the Los Muñequitos de Matanzas concert program.

Havana and Matanzas: compare the performances

Havana / La Habana

Havana-area communities, including Regla, are central to the institution’s early Cuban history. For a documented listening example, Smithsonian Folkways lists “Abakua,” performed by Abakuá members in Havana, on Folk Music of Cuba.

Listening task: follow the bell and supporting drums, then notice how the leading drum and vocal phrases move around them.

Matanzas

Matanzas is another major center, with its own musicians and performance practices. Ivor Miller discusses Los Muñequitos de Matanzas’ “Ritmo Abakuá,” recorded in 1956, as a musical greeting from Matanzas to the first Havana-area lodge.

Listening task: compare the accompaniment and the relationship between solo voice and chorus. A commercial recording is one interpretation, not a complete account of a city’s tradition.

What differs? Scholarly descriptions identify regional differences in accompanying drum parts, including biankomé and kuchí yeremá. Miller also emphasizes that Havana and Matanzas share broadly similar rhythmic practices. Compare a named performer, recording and setting; do not treat a single tempo or drum transcription as a rule for every performance in either city.

Region, lineage and occasion are different questions

“Havana” and “Matanzas” describe regional contexts. Efí and Efó refer to lineages, and Nyánkue refers to funerary music. These labels should not be treated as interchangeable. In his discussion of musical styles, Miller distinguishes Efí, Efó and Nyánkue; the last is associated with a slower, mourning context. That distinction is more informative than calling all Matanzas music slow and all Havana music fast.

The ensemble and its conversation

The biankomeko includes the leading bonkó enchemiyá and three smaller supporting drums: biankomé, kuchí yeremá and obiapá. Ekón and erikundí add bell and rattle voices. Names and spellings vary across sources. Listen to the instruments as interacting roles, not as interchangeable congas; a three-conga teaching arrangement is an adaptation.

Public music and community protocols: Abakuá music appears in commercial recordings and staged presentations, and its influence extends into rumba and popular music. These public forms do not make all ceremonial knowledge or recordings open for reuse. Keep the institutional, ceremonial and concert contexts distinct.

Pillar 4 — The Father of Salsa
Son Cubano
Son cubano is a genre of music and dance that originated in the highlands of eastern Cuba (Oriente Province, Sierra Maestra) during the late 19th century. A genre shaped by interacting African-derived and Spanish-derived musical practices. Early ancestors include changüí, nengón, and kiribá.
  • Sexteto format (~1920): tres (Cuban guitar), guitar, string bass, bongós, claves, maracas. The Sexteto Habanero (founded 1920) was the pioneering group.
  • Septeto format (late 1920s): added trumpet to the sexteto, creating the classic son ensemble.
  • Son and rumba developed through overlapping communities and exchanges; clave became central to both, with different performance practices.
  • Son became Cuba's most important 20th-century genre and a symbol of national identity — the direct ancestor of salsa.

Batá — drums in a living Lucumí tradition

Batá are hourglass-shaped drums played as a three-drum ensemble. In Cuban Lucumí religious practice, their music accompanies songs and ceremonial relationships with the orishas. This is a living community tradition, not simply a collection of rhythms for other genres.

  • Iyá: leads with phrases and cues.
  • Itótele: responds to the iyá and participates in their musical conversation.
  • Okónkolo: often maintains a repeating support pattern. Roles can change with the toque, or rhythmic setting.

Sacred and secular use: Añá-consecrated drums carry religious obligations and community protocols. Non-consecrated batá, often called aberínkula, are used in teaching and staged performance. Similar instrument shapes do not make the contexts equivalent.

Listen respectfully: start with an educational demonstration offered by a practitioner. Listen for the small drum’s repetition, then the exchange between the larger voices. Do not assume that publicly accessible ritual recordings are authorized for reuse.
