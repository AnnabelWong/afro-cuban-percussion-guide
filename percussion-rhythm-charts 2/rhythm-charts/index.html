How to read and listen

Each column lasts one eighth note. Numbers are beats; & falls halfway between beats. A dot means rest. Playback uses synthetic cues to demonstrate timing, not authentic instrument tone or hand technique. Begin slowly; BPM counts quarter-note beats.

These are newly drawn grids of selected teaching patterns, not a complete score library. Open each source for its notation, demonstrations and wider context.

Congas

Basic one-drum tumbao

One bar of 4/4. A common introductory son/salsa marcha. It is not a rumba, comparsa or Abakuá drum part. Keep the supporting heel/toe strokes lighter than the slap and open tones.

Basic one-drum tumbao · Bar 1
1&2&3&4&
HTSTHTOO

H = heel · T = toe/tips · S = slap · O = open tone

Source: Kevin Zahner · Rhythm Notes, Tumbao section ↗

Palm–tip coordination cell

One bar of 2/4. Nick Herman’s exercise 1: left, left, right, left for a right-handed player. We write S for the source’s closed-slap symbol. This is a technique exercise, not a complete traditional accompaniment.

Palm–tip coordination cell · Bar 1
1&2&
PTST

P = palm · T = tips · S = closed slap

Source: Nick Herman · Conga Control, exercise 1, p. 2 ↗

Four traditions, distinct parts.

Coverage is deliberately specific. The charts above cover selected son/salsa patterns, rumba clave and conga technique. They do not stand in for every drum part in all four traditions.

Son cubano

Start with the son clave grids and introductory conga tumbao. The timbales cáscara example belongs to the wider dance-band/salsa teaching context; not every son ensemble uses timbales.
