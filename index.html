Historical development — overlapping paths

Read left to right across the shared periods. Each lane follows a different musical setting; all three coexist. Periods are approximate, and the milestones do not mark the end of earlier traditions.

Shared background: before and during the nineteenth century

Forced migration under slavery brought diverse West and Central African peoples to Cuba. Their communities preserved and reshaped musical practices in interaction with Spanish-derived traditions and local life. There is no single African source for every Cuban rhythm.

Community & religious traditions

1800s

Abakuá, batá & rumba

Regla’s first Abakuá lodge is associated with 1836. Rumba develops in nineteenth-century Havana and Matanzas. Lucumí batá has its own religious history.

1900s–1930s

Local traditions continue

Abakuá in Havana and Matanzas, Lucumí drumming, rumba and carnival comparsas develop within their communities.

1940s–1950s

Living traditions

Ceremonial and community performance continues alongside expanding commercial music. These practices are not replaced by dance bands.

1960s–1970s

Stage & community

Folkloric presentations bring selected traditions to new audiences while religious and community contexts remain distinct.

1980s–present

Continuity & new settings

Practitioners sustain and teach these traditions in Cuba and beyond. Public performances do not make all ritual knowledge open for reuse.

Popular & dance music

1800s

Son & danzón emerge

Son develops in eastern Cuba. Danzón is established in the late nineteenth century; 1879 is a commonly cited landmark, not an origin date for all Cuban music.

1900s–1930s

Son reaches wider audiences

Son is popularized in the 1920s. Sexteto and septeto ensembles help define its recorded sound; danzón and charanga develop alongside it.

1940s–1950s

Mambo & dance orchestras

Cuban and New York dance bands expand the roles of congas, bongó, timbales and bells. Mambo reaches large international audiences.

1960s–1970s

Salsa takes shape

New York’s Cuban, Puerto Rican and other musicians reshape son, mambo and related influences into the music identified as salsa.

1980s–present

Many continuing branches

Salsa and Cuban dance music keep developing. Earlier genres remain active repertoires, rather than disappearing into one successor.

Jazz & electronic collaborations

1800s

Earlier foundations

African and Caribbean musical histories provide important background. Afro-Cuban jazz and electronic collaborations belong to later periods.

1900s–1930s

Musicians cross borders

Cuban musicians working between Cuba and the United States help create the conditions for later jazz collaborations.

1940s–1950s

Afro-Cuban jazz / Cubop

Machito and Mario Bauzá are key figures. In 1947, Chano Pozo joins Dizzy Gillespie’s band: a concrete landmark in Cuban–bebop collaboration.

1960s–1970s

Jazz experiments expand

Artists explore Cuban rhythms within changing jazz settings. These collaborations coexist with salsa and community traditions.

1980s–present

Electronic partnerships

Projects such as Ariwo and MANANA connect Cuban musicians with electronic production. This is one documented branch, not the origin of all house or Afro house.

Connections to listen for

Son and related Cuban dance styles → salsa: a major musical foundation, alongside other Caribbean and U.S. influences.

Cuban percussion + bebop → Cubop: a documented collaboration involving musicians such as Chano Pozo and Dizzy Gillespie.

Cuban practitioners + electronic producers: artist-specific collaborations, including Ariwo. Similar sounds alone do not establish a historical lineage.

Optional resources for sharing this guide

Optional: five-post thread drafts

Use these short drafts as starting points alongside the lessons above.

Post 1

1/5 — Clave gives many Afro-Cuban grooves a shared rhythmic reference. Son and rumba clave each have five strokes, arranged as a three-side and a two-side. Listen, count and compare them.

Post 2

2/5 — Explore rumba, carnival comparsas, the Havana and Matanzas traditions of Abakuá, and son cubano, then learn about batá in the distinct Lucumí tradition. Their histories intersect, but their instruments and cultural contexts are not interchangeable.

Post 3

3/5 — Congas, bongó and Cuban cajones offer contrasting hand-drum voices. Listen for repeating support parts, changes of tone and the moments when a player answers or improvises.

Post 4

4/5 — Timbales, cowbells, chekeré and güiro add shell sounds, metallic patterns, shakes and scrapes. Hear how the parts fit together without all playing the same rhythm.

Post 5

5/5 — Cuban percussion has important connections with salsa and Afro-Cuban jazz. Some electronic music also uses related sounds and figures, but not every track follows clave. Follow specific recordings and musicians to hear the connections.

Content Guidelines

  • Cultural sensitivity: For the batá lesson, frame Santería/Lucumí as a living religious tradition. Avoid sensationalizing "secret rituals" or spirit possession. Clearly distinguish sacred batá (Añá-consecrated) from non-consecrated/aberínkula batá. Do not use ritual footage or audio without explicit permission from the religious community.
  • Terminology accuracy: Use both Spanish and Yoruba/Lucumí terms where applicable (e.g., "conga / tumbadora," "bongó," "cencerro / campana"). Provide brief glosses for each.
  • Clave direction: Always specify whether a pattern is 3-2 or 2-3 when discussing specific songs or genres.
  • African origins: Say "West and Central African" unless a specific tradition is identified (e.g., Yoruba, Bantu/Kongo, Arará/Ewe-Fon, Abakuá/Ékpè).
  • Posting cadence: One post per day or every other day works well. Each post should be self-contained but reward readers who follow the full sequence.
  • Engagement: Encourage saves and shares. Ask followers to name their favorite Afro-Cuban drummer or track in the comments of Post 5.
